\section{Setting}

We study the Gabrovski--Silva (GS) economy with one change: the destruction rate $\delta_t$
and the separation probability $s_t$ are time-varying shocks. In the draft this is the AGS economy of \texttt{prop:ags} with
$\sigma=\kappa=0$, which \texttt{rem:nesting} identifies as GS extended with aggregate
shocks to $\delta_t$ and $s_t$. Its labor-market block closes on its own, as under the
conditions of \texttt{prop:independence}. The discount rate is $r$,
so the discount factor is $1/(1+r)$. Output per match, $y$ (recruiter compensation $z/\mu$
in our model), is constant. Exit is exogenous, so a business survives from $t$ to $t+1$
with probability $1-\delta_t$. Every vacated position is reposted at no cost, and there are
no flow recruiting costs, so all recruiting costs are sunk.

\paragraph{Shock processes}
\[
	\delta_t=\bar\delta\exp(\tilde\delta_t),
	\qquad
	s_t=\bar s\exp(\tilde s_t),
\]
where $\tilde\delta_t$ and $\tilde s_t$ are mean-zero AR(1) processes and are the log
deviations of $\delta_t$ and $s_t$. We study one shock at a time and write $\rho\in[0,1)$
for the persistence of the shock under study, $\tilde x_t=\rho\,\tilde x_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t$
for $x\in\{\delta,s\}$, with $\varepsilon_t$ mean zero. The total separation rate
\[
	\tau_t\equiv1-(1-\delta_t)(1-s_t)
\]
moves with both. Its steady state is $\bar\tau=1-(1-\bar\delta)(1-\bar s)$.

\paragraph{Timing}
We follow GS (their Fig.~2) and the draft (S10). $\delta_t$ and $s_t$ are known at the
start of $t$. Entry, production and matching take place within $t$. Separation and
destruction take place at the end of $t$, at rates $s_t$ and $\delta_t$, and a match formed at $t$ is exposed
to destruction at the end of $t$ but not to separation. So $u_t$ and the vacancies
inherited from $t-1$ are fixed at $t$, while tightness $\theta_t$ and entry $e_t$ respond
at $t$.

\paragraph{Matching}
$q(\theta)=A\theta^{-\eta_L}$ and $f(\theta)=\theta q(\theta)$, with $\eta_L\in(0,1)$. GS
use the den Haan--Ramey--Watson function; we use Cobb--Douglas, as in our calibration.

\section{The nonlinear system}

\subsection{Vacancy value and flow value}

A business opportunity costs $x$, drawn from $G(x)=(x/x_m)^\xi$ on $[0,x_m]$, and is
taken if and only if $x\le Q_t$. So $e_t=G(Q_t)$ and $Q_t=e_t^{1/\xi}x_m$. Under infinitely
elastic creation ($\xi\to\infty$),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:n_fe}
	Q_t=x_m\qquad\text{for all }t .
\end{equation}
The flow value of a vacancy is defined as in GS (2), with the survival factor dated $t$:
\begin{align}\label{eq:n_K}
	K_t\equiv Q_t-\frac{1-\delta_t}{1+r}\,\mathbb{E}_tQ_{t+1}=x_m\,\frac{r+\delta_t}{1+r}.
\end{align}
Whereas $Q$ is constant, $K_t$ is a known function of the exogenous $\delta_t$. In GS
$\delta$ is constant, so free entry fixes $K$ as well (GS (13)). Here $\delta_t$ is the
depreciation rate of the vacancy asset, so its flow value moves with $\delta_t$. Holding
$K$ fixed would give a flow-cost DMP model (Remark~\ref{rem:flow}). We substitute
\eqref{eq:n_K} wherever $K$ appears.

\subsection{Wage and job creation}

The Nash wage is GS (7):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:n_w}
	w_t=\phi\bigl[y-K_t+\theta_tK_t\bigr]+(1-\phi)b .
\end{equation}
The job creation condition is GS (10), with the survival factor dated $t$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:n_jcc_gs}
	\frac{K_t}{q(\theta_t)}
	=\frac{1-\delta_t}{1+r}\,\mathbb{E}_t\Bigl[y-w_{t+1}-K_{t+1}+(1-s_{t+1})\frac{K_{t+1}}{q(\theta_{t+1})}\Bigr].
\end{equation}
The left side is the expected cost of a hire: the flow value of a vacancy times the
expected time to fill. The right side is the expected profit from a match, which first
produces at $t+1$, and only if the business survives destruction at the end of $t$. Profit
has two parts: the flow profit net of the forgone vacancy value, $y-w_{t+1}-K_{t+1}$, and
the hiring cost saved if the match continues, $(1-s_{t+1})K_{t+1}/q(\theta_{t+1})$. A match
formed at $t$ first faces separation at the end of $t+1$, so the relevant rate is
$s_{t+1}$. The wage \eqref{eq:n_w} does not depend on $s$: the separation terms in the
firm's and the worker's surplus cancel in the Nash sharing rule.

Using the Nash wage \eqref{eq:n_w} dated $t+1$,
\[
	y-w_{t+1}-K_{t+1}=(1-\phi)(y-b)-K_{t+1}\bigl[(1-\phi)+\phi\,\theta_{t+1}\bigr].
\]
So the term in brackets in \eqref{eq:n_jcc_gs} is
\[
	(1-\phi)(y-b)+K_{t+1}\Bigl[\frac{1-s_{t+1}}{q(\theta_{t+1})}-\phi\,\theta_{t+1}-(1-\phi)\Bigr].
\]
Multiply \eqref{eq:n_jcc_gs} by $1+r$ and substitute \eqref{eq:n_K} at $t$ and $t+1$. The
job creation condition becomes an equation in $\theta$ and the two shocks alone:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:n_jcc}
	\frac{x_m(r+\delta_t)}{q(\theta_t)}
	=(1-\delta_t)\,\mathbb{E}_t\Bigl\{(1-\phi)(y-b)
	+\frac{x_m(r+\delta_{t+1})}{1+r}\Bigl[\frac{1-s_{t+1}}{q(\theta_{t+1})}-\phi\,\theta_{t+1}-(1-\phi)\Bigr]\Bigr\}.
\end{equation}
The left-hand side represents the average hiring cost. The right-hand side represents the
benefit of a filled job. Its first term, $(1-\phi)(y-b)$, is the firm's share of the match's
flow surplus. In the second term, $x_m(r+\delta_{t+1})/(1+r)=K_{t+1}$ is next period's flow
value of a vacancy. It is multiplied by $(1-s_{t+1})/q(\theta_{t+1})$, the hiring cost saved if
the match continues; by $-\phi\,\theta_{t+1}$, the worker's share of that saving,
$\phi fK/q$; and by $-(1-\phi)$, the firm's share of the forgone flow value of the vacancy.

\subsection{Laws of motion}

Vacancies follow GS (8). Unemployment follows GS (9), with $\delta$ and $\tau$ dated $t-1$
because the destruction they cause takes place at the end of $t-1$:
\begin{align}
	v_t&=(1-\delta_{t-1})\bigl[1-q(\theta_{t-1})\bigr]v_{t-1}+(1-\delta_{t-1})s_{t-1}(1-u_{t-1})+e_t,
	\label{eq:n_v}\\
	u_t&=\bigl[1-(1-\delta_{t-1})f(\theta_{t-1})\bigr]u_{t-1}+\tau_{t-1}(1-u_{t-1}),
	\label{eq:n_u}\\
	\theta_t&=v_t/u_t. \label{eq:n_theta}
\end{align}
The vacancy pool \eqref{eq:n_v} has three parts: unmatched vacancies from $t-1$ that avoided
destruction, vacancies reposted after a separation at a surviving business, and new
entrants $e_t$. The first two are fixed before $t$. Call their sum the predetermined stock:
\[
	v_{pre,t}\equiv(1-\delta_{t-1})\Bigl\{\bigl[1-q(\theta_{t-1})\bigr]v_{t-1}+s_{t-1}(1-u_{t-1})\Bigr\},
	\qquad v_t=v_{pre,t}+e_t .
\]
Every variable on its right side is dated $t-1$, which is what makes it predetermined at
$t$. Unemployment \eqref{eq:n_u} has two parts: last period's unemployed who were not
hired, or were hired at a business that was then destroyed, and last period's employed who
separated, at rate $\tau_{t-1}$.

\paragraph{How free entry orders the system}
Equation \eqref{eq:n_jcc} contains only $\theta$ and the exogenous shocks, so it
determines the path of $\theta$ on its own. Equation \eqref{eq:n_u} then gives $u$,
\eqref{eq:n_theta} gives $v=\theta u$, and \eqref{eq:n_v} determines entry,
$e_t=v_t-v_{pre,t}$. With finitely elastic creation the causation reverses: entry is set by
$Q$, \eqref{eq:n_v} determines $v$, and \eqref{eq:n_jcc_gs} determines $Q$.

\section{Steady state}

Following GS's Definition 2, with $Q=x_m$, the steady state satisfies
\begin{align}
	\frac{\bar K}{q(\bar\theta)}&=\frac{1-\bar\delta}{r+\bar\tau}\bigl(y-\bar w-\bar K\bigr),
	\label{eq:ss_jcc}\\
	\bar K&=x_m\,\frac{r+\bar\delta}{1+r},\label{eq:ss_K}\\
	\bar w&=\phi\bigl[y-\bar K+\bar\theta\bar K\bigr]+(1-\phi)b,\label{eq:ss_w}\\
	\bar u&=\frac{\bar\tau}{\bar\tau+(1-\bar\delta)f(\bar\theta)},\label{eq:ss_u}\\
	\bar e&=\bar\delta\,(\bar v+1-\bar u),\label{eq:ss_e}\\
	\bar\theta&=\bar v/\bar u .\notag
\end{align}
These are GS (12)--(16).

Equation \eqref{eq:ss_jcc} is the steady state of \eqref{eq:n_jcc_gs},
\[
	\frac{\bar K}{\bar q}
	=\frac{1-\bar\delta}{1+r}\Bigl[y-\bar w-\bar K+(1-\bar s)\frac{\bar K}{\bar q}\Bigr],
\]
multiplied by $1+r$ and rearranged using $(1+r)-(1-\bar\delta)(1-\bar s)=r+\bar\tau$. Equation
\eqref{eq:ss_K} is a user cost: the asset price $x_m$ times the discount rate plus the
destruction rate. The share of the user cost due to destruction is $\bar\delta/(r+\bar\delta)$.
Equation \eqref{eq:ss_u} can be written $(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\bar u=\bar\tau(1-\bar u)$:
hires equal separations. Since $\bar f\bar u=\bar q\bar v$, this is also
$(1-\bar\delta)\bar q\bar v=\bar\tau(1-\bar u)$.

For \eqref{eq:ss_e}, the steady state of \eqref{eq:n_v} gives
\[
	\bar e=\bar v-(1-\bar\delta)(1-\bar q)\bar v-(1-\bar\delta)\bar s(1-\bar u)
	=\bar\delta\bar v+(1-\bar\delta)\bar q\bar v-(1-\bar\delta)\bar s(1-\bar u).
\]
Using hires equal separations, this is $\bar\delta\bar v+[\bar\tau-(1-\bar\delta)\bar s](1-\bar u)$,
and $\bar\tau-(1-\bar\delta)\bar s=\bar\delta$. So entry replaces the positions destroyed, both
vacant ($\bar v$) and filled ($1-\bar u$). Positions vacated by separations are reposted
and need no replacement. The shares of entrants and of predetermined vacancies in the stock
are therefore
\[
	\frac{\bar e}{\bar v}=\frac{\bar\delta(\bar v+1-\bar u)}{\bar v},
	\qquad
	\frac{\bar v_{pre}}{\bar v}=1-\frac{\bar\delta(\bar v+1-\bar u)}{\bar v}.
\]
Filled positions far outnumber vacancies, $(1-\bar u)/\bar v\approx26$ at our targets, so
the entrant share is small when destruction is rare. Hires equal separations gives
$(1-\bar u)/\bar v=(1-\bar\delta)\bar q/\bar\tau$: filled jobs per vacancy equal hires per
vacancy times the expected duration of a job. An equivalent form of the entrant share is
therefore
\[
	\frac{\bar e}{\bar v}=\frac{\bar\delta}{\bar\tau}\bigl[\bar\tau+(1-\bar\delta)\bar q\bigr].
\]
Our calibration fixes $\bar\tau$ and the gross vacancy-filling rate $(1-\bar\delta)\bar q$, so
the bracket is the same, $0.831$, in every economy we compare. The entrant share is then
proportional to the destruction share of separations, $\bar\delta/\bar\tau$.

\section{Log-linear system}

Hats denote log deviations from the steady state, $\hat x_t\equiv\ln x_t-\ln\bar x$. The
shocks are already in logs, $\hat\delta_t=\tilde\delta_t$ and $\hat s_t=\tilde s_t$, so
$\delta_t-\bar\delta\approx\bar\delta\,\tilde\delta_t$ and $s_t-\bar s\approx\bar s\,\tilde s_t$.
The total separation rate moves by
$\tau_t-\bar\tau=(1-\bar s)(\delta_t-\bar\delta)+(1-\bar\delta)(s_t-\bar s)$, so
\[
	\hat\tau_t=(1-\bar s)\,\frac{\bar\delta}{\bar\tau}\,\tilde\delta_t
	+\Bigl(1-\frac{\bar\delta}{\bar\tau}\Bigr)\tilde s_t ,
\]
using $(1-\bar\delta)\bar s=\bar\tau-\bar\delta$. A one-percent rise in $\delta$ raises $\tau$
by $(1-\bar s)\bar\delta/\bar\tau$ percent, the destruction share of separations up to the
factor $1-\bar s$. A one-percent rise in $s$ raises it by $1-\bar\delta/\bar\tau$ percent, the
separation share.

\subsection{Job creation}

Take logs of both sides of \eqref{eq:n_jcc}. On the left,
\[
	\ln\frac{x_m(r+\delta_t)}{q(\theta_t)}
	=\ln x_m+\ln\bigl(r+\bar\delta e^{\tilde\delta_t}\bigr)-\ln A+\eta_L\ln\theta_t ,
\]
whose first-order deviation is
\[
	\frac{\bar\delta}{r+\bar\delta}\,\tilde\delta_t+\eta_L\hat\theta_t .
\]

On the right, the log of $1-\delta_t$ deviates by $-[\bar\delta/(1-\bar\delta)]\tilde\delta_t$.
Write $\Sigma_{t+1}$ for the term in braces in \eqref{eq:n_jcc}. In the steady state of
\eqref{eq:n_jcc}, $x_m(r+\bar\delta)/\bar q=(1-\bar\delta)\bar\Sigma$. Only the second term
of $\Sigma_{t+1}$ varies, and its deviation is
\[
	d\Sigma_{t+1}=\frac{x_m}{1+r}\Bigl\{\bar\delta\,\tilde\delta_{t+1}
	\Bigl[\frac{1-\bar s}{\bar q}-\phi\bar\theta-(1-\phi)\Bigr]
	+(r+\bar\delta)\Bigl[(1-\bar s)\frac{\eta_L}{\bar q}-\phi\bar\theta\Bigr]\hat\theta_{t+1}
	-(r+\bar\delta)\frac{\bar s}{\bar q}\,\tilde s_{t+1}\Bigr\},
\]
using $d(1/q)=(\eta_L/\bar q)\hat\theta$, $d\theta=\bar\theta\hat\theta$ and
$ds=\bar s\tilde s$. Divide by $\bar\Sigma=x_m(r+\bar\delta)/[(1-\bar\delta)\bar q]$, and use
$\bar\theta\bar q=\bar f$:
\[
	\hat\Sigma_{t+1}=\frac{1-\bar\delta}{1+r}\Bigl\{\bigl[1-\bar s-\phi\bar f-(1-\phi)\bar q\bigr]\frac{\bar\delta}{r+\bar\delta}\,\tilde\delta_{t+1}
	+\bigl[(1-\bar s)\eta_L-\phi\bar f\bigr]\hat\theta_{t+1}-\bar s\,\tilde s_{t+1}\Bigr\}.
\]
Distribute $1-\bar\delta$ inside the braces. Since $(1-\bar\delta)(1-\bar s)=1-\bar\tau$,
\[
	\hat\Sigma_{t+1}=\frac{1}{1+r}\Bigl\{\bigl[1-\bar\tau-\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f-(1-\phi)(1-\bar\delta)\bar q\bigr]\frac{\bar\delta}{r+\bar\delta}\,\tilde\delta_{t+1}
	+\bigl[(1-\bar\tau)\eta_L-\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\bigr]\hat\theta_{t+1}
	-(1-\bar\delta)\bar s\,\tilde s_{t+1}\Bigr\}.
\]
The matching rates now appear as $(1-\bar\delta)\bar f$ and $(1-\bar\delta)\bar q$, the
effective job-finding and vacancy-filling rates: the probabilities that a match forms and
its business survives to production. These are the rates we calibrate, $0.41$ and $0.80$.
Setting the left-side deviation equal to
$-[\bar\delta/(1-\bar\delta)]\tilde\delta_t+\mathbb{E}_t\hat\Sigma_{t+1}$, moving the
$\tilde\delta_t$ term to the right and collecting terms,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l_jcc}
	\boxed{\;
	\eta_L\hat\theta_t
	=-\Bigl[\frac{\bar\delta}{r+\bar\delta}+\frac{\bar\delta}{1-\bar\delta}\Bigr]\tilde\delta_t
	+\frac{1}{1+r}\,\mathbb{E}_t\Bigl\{\bigl[1-\bar\tau-\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f-(1-\phi)(1-\bar\delta)\bar q\bigr]\frac{\bar\delta}{r+\bar\delta}\,\tilde\delta_{t+1}
	+\bigl[(1-\bar\tau)\eta_L-\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\bigr]\hat\theta_{t+1}
	-(1-\bar\delta)\bar s\,\tilde s_{t+1}\Bigr\}\;}
\end{equation}
Only $\hat\theta$ and the shocks appear. Next period's terms are discounted at $1/(1+r)$,
and survival enters through the probability that the match continues, $1-\bar\tau$, and
through the effective matching rates.

The weights in \eqref{eq:l_jcc} read as follows. The coefficient $\eta_L$ on $\hat\theta_t$
says that a tighter market lengthens the time to fill, so a hire costs more. The weight
$\bar\delta/(r+\bar\delta)$ on $\tilde\delta_t$ is the flow-value effect: a one-percent rise
in $\delta$ raises the flow value $K_t=x_m(r+\delta_t)/(1+r)$ by $\bar\delta/(r+\bar\delta)$
percent, the share of the user cost due to destruction, and so raises the cost of a hire.
This share is close to one when $\bar\delta\gg r$ and close to $\bar\delta/r$ when
$\bar\delta\ll r$. The weight $\bar\delta/(1-\bar\delta)$ is the survival effect: a hire pays
off only if the business survives, so destruction lowers the value of a hire. It is about
$\bar\delta$, which is small.

The terms in $\tilde\delta_{t+1}$ are nonzero only if the shock persists. A higher flow
value next period raises the hiring cost saved by a match that continues, with probability
$1-\bar\tau$. Part of that saving goes to the worker through her outside option,
$-\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f$. A higher flow value also lowers the firm's flow profit, since a
matched firm forgoes it, $-(1-\phi)(1-\bar\delta)\bar q$. In the same way, a tighter market
next period raises the hiring cost a continuing match saves, $(1-\bar\tau)\eta_L$, but it
also raises the worker's outside option and hence the wage, $-\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f$.

The separation shock enters only through $-(1-\bar\delta)\bar s\,\tilde s_{t+1}$. A higher
separation rate next period lowers the probability that a match continues, and with it the
hiring cost the match saves. Unlike $\delta$, $s$ does not move the flow value of a vacancy,
because $K_t$ depends only on $\delta_t$. And because the term is dated $t+1$, an iid
separation shock does not move tightness at all.

\subsection{Unemployment}

Write the right side of \eqref{eq:n_u} as
$U(u_{t-1},\delta_{t-1},\tau_{t-1},\theta_{t-1})
=[1-(1-\delta_{t-1})A\theta_{t-1}^{1-\eta_L}]u_{t-1}+\tau_{t-1}(1-u_{t-1})$. Its partial
derivatives at the steady state are
\begin{align*}
	\frac{\partial U}{\partial u_{t-1}}&=1-(1-\bar\delta)\bar f-\bar\tau,
	&\frac{\partial U}{\partial\delta_{t-1}}&=\bar f\,\bar u,\\
	\frac{\partial U}{\partial\tau_{t-1}}&=1-\bar u,
	&\frac{\partial U}{\partial\theta_{t-1}}&=-(1-\bar\delta)\,\bar u\,(1-\eta_L)\frac{\bar f}{\bar\theta}.
\end{align*}
Hence, with $\delta_{t-1}-\bar\delta=\bar\delta\tilde\delta_{t-1}$ and
$\tau_{t-1}-\bar\tau=\bar\tau\hat\tau_{t-1}$,
\[
	u_t-\bar u=\bigl[1-(1-\bar\delta)\bar f-\bar\tau\bigr](u_{t-1}-\bar u)
	+\bar f\bar u\,\bar\delta\,\tilde\delta_{t-1}+(1-\bar u)\bar\tau\,\hat\tau_{t-1}
	-(1-\bar\delta)\bar u(1-\eta_L)\bar f\,\hat\theta_{t-1}.
\]
Divide by $\bar u$, and use $(1-\bar u)\bar\tau/\bar u=(1-\bar\delta)\bar f$ from
\eqref{eq:ss_u}. Define
\[
	\lambda\equiv1-(1-\bar\delta)\bar f-\bar\tau,
	\qquad
	\omega\equiv(1-\bar\delta)\bar f(1-\eta_L).
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l_u_tau}
	\hat u_t=\lambda\,\hat u_{t-1}+\bar f\bar\delta\,\tilde\delta_{t-1}
	+(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\,\hat\tau_{t-1}-\omega\,\hat\theta_{t-1}.
\end{equation}
Substitute $\hat\tau_{t-1}$. The two coefficients on $\tilde\delta_{t-1}$ combine as
\[
	\bar f\bar\delta+(1-\bar\delta)(1-\bar s)\bar f\,\frac{\bar\delta}{\bar\tau}
	=\bar f\,\frac{\bar\delta}{\bar\tau}\bigl[\bar\tau+(1-\bar\tau)\bigr]
	=\bar f\,\frac{\bar\delta}{\bar\tau},
\]
because $(1-\bar\delta)(1-\bar s)=1-\bar\tau$, and the coefficient on $\tilde s_{t-1}$ is
$(1-\bar\delta)\bar f(1-\bar\delta/\bar\tau)$. So
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l_u}
	\boxed{\;\hat u_t=\lambda\,\hat u_{t-1}+\bar f\,\frac{\bar\delta}{\bar\tau}\,\tilde\delta_{t-1}
	+(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\Bigl(1-\frac{\bar\delta}{\bar\tau}\Bigr)\tilde s_{t-1}
	-\omega\,\hat\theta_{t-1}\;}
\end{equation}

The coefficient $\lambda$ is persistence. An extra unemployed worker is still unemployed
next month unless hired at a surviving business, with probability $1-(1-\bar\delta)\bar f$,
and the $\bar\tau$ term reflects that one fewer employed worker means one fewer separation.
The destruction weight has two channels, visible in \eqref{eq:l_u_tau}. The first, $\bar
f\bar\delta$, is lost matches: new matches at a destroyed business never produce. The
second is separations. In steady state the inflow into unemployment equals the outflow,
$(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\bar u$, so a one-percent rise in separations raises unemployment by
$(1-\bar\delta)\bar f$ percent, and separations rise by $(1-\bar s)\bar\delta/\bar\tau$ percent per
percent of $\delta$. Together the two channels give $\bar f\bar\delta/\bar\tau$, and the
second dominates. The unemployment loading is therefore proportional to the destruction
share of separations, $\bar\delta/\bar\tau$. The separation weight,
$(1-\bar\delta)\bar f(1-\bar\delta/\bar\tau)$, has only the second channel, since $s$ does not
hit matches before they produce. It is the effective job-finding rate times the separation
share of separations. Finally, $\omega$ is the hiring channel: lower tightness at $t-1$
means fewer hires, and those workers are still unemployed at $t$.

\subsection{Vacancies}

Take the deviation of $v_t=v_{pre,t}+e_t$ and divide by $\bar v$:
\[
	\hat v_t=\frac{\bar v_{pre}}{\bar v}\,\hat v_{pre,t}+\frac{\bar e}{\bar v}\,\hat e_t .
\]
Substituting the shares implied by \eqref{eq:ss_e},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l_v}
	\boxed{\;
	\hat v_t=\Bigl[1-\frac{\bar\delta(\bar v+1-\bar u)}{\bar v}\Bigr]\hat v_{pre,t}
	+\frac{\bar\delta(\bar v+1-\bar u)}{\bar v}\,\hat e_t\;}
\end{equation}

For the predetermined stock, the partial derivatives of
$v_{pre,t}=(1-\delta_{t-1})\{[1-A\theta_{t-1}^{-\eta_L}]v_{t-1}+s_{t-1}(1-u_{t-1})\}$ at the
steady state are
\begin{align*}
	\frac{\partial v_{pre,t}}{\partial\delta_{t-1}}&=-\bigl[(1-\bar q)\bar v+\bar s(1-\bar u)\bigr]
	=-\frac{\bar v_{pre}}{1-\bar\delta},
	&\frac{\partial v_{pre,t}}{\partial v_{t-1}}&=(1-\bar\delta)(1-\bar q),\\
	\frac{\partial v_{pre,t}}{\partial\theta_{t-1}}&=(1-\bar\delta)\,\eta_L\frac{\bar q}{\bar\theta}\,\bar v,
	&\frac{\partial v_{pre,t}}{\partial u_{t-1}}&=-(1-\bar\delta)\bar s,\\
	\frac{\partial v_{pre,t}}{\partial s_{t-1}}&=(1-\bar\delta)(1-\bar u).
\end{align*}
The first uses the steady state $\bar v_{pre}=(1-\bar\delta)[(1-\bar q)\bar v+\bar s(1-\bar u)]$,
and the third uses $\partial q/\partial\theta=-\eta_L\bar q/\bar\theta$. Hence, with
$\delta_{t-1}-\bar\delta=\bar\delta\tilde\delta_{t-1}$ and $s_{t-1}-\bar s=\bar s\tilde s_{t-1}$,
\[
	v_{pre,t}-\bar v_{pre}
	=-\frac{\bar v_{pre}\,\bar\delta}{1-\bar\delta}\,\tilde\delta_{t-1}
	+(1-\bar\delta)\bigl[(1-\bar q)\bar v\,\hat v_{t-1}+\eta_L\bar q\bar v\,\hat\theta_{t-1}
	-\bar s\bar u\,\hat u_{t-1}+\bar s(1-\bar u)\,\tilde s_{t-1}\bigr].
\]
Dividing by $\bar v_{pre}=\bar v-\bar e$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l_vpre}
	\boxed{\;
	\hat v_{pre,t}=-\frac{\bar\delta}{1-\bar\delta}\,\tilde\delta_{t-1}
	+\frac{1-\bar\delta}{\bar v-\bar e}\Bigl[(1-\bar q)\bar v\,\hat v_{t-1}
	+\eta_L\bar q\bar v\,\hat\theta_{t-1}-\bar s\bar u\,\hat u_{t-1}
	+\bar s(1-\bar u)\,\tilde s_{t-1}\Bigr]\;}
\end{equation}

Entry replaces the positions destroyed, so its weight in \eqref{eq:l_v},
$\bar\delta(\bar v+1-\bar u)/\bar v$, is proportional to $\bar\delta$: $0.073$ at D1 and
$0.831$ at $\bar\delta=\bar\tau$. The predetermined stock is the rest, $0.927$ and $0.169$.
In \eqref{eq:l_vpre}, a one-percent rise in $\delta_{t-1}$ destroys about $\bar\delta$
percent of the predetermined stock, while entrants posted at $t$ are not exposed. The
bracket collects the changes in the sources of the predetermined stock, in units of
vacancies. More vacancies at $t-1$ leave more unfilled ones ($(1-\bar q)\bar v\,\hat v_{t-1}$).
A tighter market fills fewer of them ($\eta_L\bar q\bar v\,\hat\theta_{t-1}$); after a
destruction shock $\hat\theta<0$, so vacancies fill faster and fewer carry over. And reposts
come from the employed, who are fewer when $u$ is higher ($-\bar s\bar u\,\hat u_{t-1}$), and
they rise one for one with the separation rate ($\bar s(1-\bar u)\,\tilde s_{t-1}$). A
separation shock thus adds the same positions to the vacancy pool that it adds workers to
unemployment, which is the accounting behind Prop.~5, Part~1.

\paragraph{At free entry}
With $\hat v_{t-1}=\hat\theta_{t-1}+\hat u_{t-1}$, the bracket in \eqref{eq:l_vpre}
becomes $(1-\bar q+\eta_L\bar q)\bar v\,\hat\theta_{t-1}+[(1-\bar q)\bar v-\bar s\bar u]\hat u_{t-1}
+\bar s(1-\bar u)\,\tilde s_{t-1}$.
Equation \eqref{eq:l_v} then determines entry, not the vacancy stock:
\[
	\hat e_t=\frac{\bar v}{\bar\delta(\bar v+1-\bar u)}
	\Bigl\{\hat v_t-\Bigl[1-\frac{\bar\delta(\bar v+1-\bar u)}{\bar v}\Bigr]\hat v_{pre,t}\Bigr\},
	\qquad \hat v_t=\hat\theta_t+\hat u_t .
\]
When the entrant weight is small, as at D1, a given gap between the target stock and the
predetermined stock requires a large percentage change in entry.

\end{equation}

\section{Proposition}

With $\lambda$ and $\omega$ as defined in Section~4, let $\rho$ be the persistence of the
shock under study and define the common denominator
\[
	D\equiv\eta_L\bigl[1+r-\rho(1-\bar\tau)\bigr]+\rho\,\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f
\]
and the tightness coefficients
\begin{equation}\label{eq:a}
	a\equiv-\,\frac{(1+r)\dfrac{\bar\delta}{1-\bar\delta}
	+\dfrac{\bar\delta}{r+\bar\delta}\Bigl[1+r-\rho(1-\bar\tau)+\rho\,\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f
	+\rho\,(1-\phi)(1-\bar\delta)\bar q\Bigr]}{D},
	\qquad
	b\equiv-\,\frac{\rho\,(1-\bar\delta)\bar s}{D}.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Tightness under infinitely elastic vacancy creation]\label{lem:theta}
Assume the setting of Section~1 with $\xi\to\infty$, and suppose
$\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f<\eta_L(2+r-\bar\tau)$, which holds whenever $\phi\le\eta_L$. Then the
log-linear system \eqref{eq:l_jcc}--\eqref{eq:l_theta} has a unique bounded solution, in
which tightness depends only on the shocks:
\[
	\hat\theta_t=a\,\tilde\delta_t+b\,\tilde s_t,\qquad a<0,\quad b\le0,
\]
with $a$ and $b$ evaluated at the persistence of the respective shock, and $b=0$ if and only
if $\rho=0$. The endogenous states $u_t$ and $v_{pre,t}$ do not enter the policy rule for
tightness.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A general linear policy rule would take the form
$\hat\theta_t=a\,\tilde\delta_t+b\,\tilde s_t+b_u\hat u_t+b_v\hat v_{pre,t}$. Equation
\eqref{eq:l_jcc} contains neither $u$ nor $v_{pre}$, because free entry pins $Q$ and
$K_t=x_m(r+\delta_t)/(1+r)$ depends only on the exogenous $\delta_t$. Dividing it by
$\eta_L$,
\[
	\hat\theta_t=\Phi\,\mathbb{E}_t\hat\theta_{t+1}+c_0\tilde\delta_t+c_1\mathbb{E}_t\tilde\delta_{t+1}
	+c_2\mathbb{E}_t\tilde s_{t+1},
	\qquad
	\Phi\equiv\frac{1}{1+r}\Bigl[1-\bar\tau-\frac{\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f}{\eta_L}\Bigr],
\]
\[
	c_0\equiv-\frac{1}{\eta_L}\Bigl[\frac{\bar\delta}{r+\bar\delta}+\frac{\bar\delta}{1-\bar\delta}\Bigr],
	\quad
	c_1\equiv\frac{1-\bar\tau-\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f-(1-\phi)(1-\bar\delta)\bar q}{\eta_L(1+r)}\,
	\frac{\bar\delta}{r+\bar\delta},
	\quad
	c_2\equiv-\frac{(1-\bar\delta)\bar s}{\eta_L(1+r)}.
\]
Iterating forward $T$ periods,
\[
	\hat\theta_t=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\Phi^k\,\mathbb{E}_t\bigl(c_0\tilde\delta_{t+k}+c_1\tilde\delta_{t+k+1}
	+c_2\tilde s_{t+k+1}\bigr)+\Phi^T\,\mathbb{E}_t\hat\theta_{t+T}.
\]
Here $\Phi<(1-\bar\tau)/(1+r)<1$. And $\Phi>-1$ if and only if
$1-\bar\tau-\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f/\eta_L>-(1+r)$, that is,
$\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f<\eta_L(2+r-\bar\tau)$, which is the assumption. It holds whenever
$\phi\le\eta_L$, because $(1-\bar\delta)\bar f<1<2+r-\bar\tau$. For a bounded solution the
last term vanishes as $T\to\infty$, so the unique bounded solution is the forward sum. It
depends only on current and expected future shocks, so $b_u=b_v=0$. With each shock an
AR(1), every term of the sum is proportional to the current shock, so the rule is linear
in $\tilde\delta_t$ and $\tilde s_t$.

To find $a$, set $\tilde s\equiv0$, substitute $\hat\theta_t=a\tilde\delta_t$,
$\mathbb{E}_t\hat\theta_{t+1}=a\rho\tilde\delta_t$ and
$\mathbb{E}_t\tilde\delta_{t+1}=\rho\tilde\delta_t$ into \eqref{eq:l_jcc}, divide by
$\tilde\delta_t$ and multiply by $1+r$:
\begin{multline*}
	(1+r)\,\eta_L a=-(1+r)\Bigl[\frac{\bar\delta}{r+\bar\delta}+\frac{\bar\delta}{1-\bar\delta}\Bigr]
	+\rho\bigl[1-\bar\tau-\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f-(1-\phi)(1-\bar\delta)\bar q\bigr]\frac{\bar\delta}{r+\bar\delta}\\
	+\rho\bigl[(1-\bar\tau)\eta_L-\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\bigr]a .
\end{multline*}
Collecting the terms in $a$ on the left gives $aD$ on the left and the negative of the
numerator of $a$ on the right, which is \eqref{eq:a}. To find $b$, set
$\tilde\delta\equiv0$ and substitute $\hat\theta_t=b\tilde s_t$ and
$\mathbb{E}_t\tilde s_{t+1}=\rho\tilde s_t$ in the same way:
\[
	(1+r)\,\eta_L b=-\rho(1-\bar\delta)\bar s+\rho\bigl[(1-\bar\tau)\eta_L-\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\bigr]b,
\]
so $bD=-\rho(1-\bar\delta)\bar s$. Since $\rho<1$, $1+r-\rho(1-\bar\tau)>0$, so $D>0$ and every
term of the numerator of $a$ is positive. Hence $a<0$, and $b\le0$ with equality if and only
if $\rho=0$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Reading \eqref{eq:a}}
The term common to the two denominators,
\[
	1+r-\rho(1-\bar\tau)=r+\bar\tau+(1-\rho)(1-\bar\tau),
\]
is the rate at which a match discounts the shock's effects on its future payoffs: the
discount rate, plus the separation rate, plus the rate at which the shock dies out. In $D$
it multiplies $\eta_L$, the response of the expected cost of a hire to tightness. The second
term of $D$, $\rho\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f$, is the wage feedback: lower tightness lowers the
worker's effective job-finding rate and hence her outside option, which props up the firm's
surplus. At $\rho=1$, $D=\eta_L(r+\bar\tau)+\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f$, the same combination of
$r+\bar\tau$ and $\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f$ that appears in the steady-state job creation
condition once the wage is substituted.

The numerator of $a$ has a survival part, $(1+r)\bar\delta/(1-\bar\delta)$, which is small,
and a flow-value part, $\bar\delta/(r+\bar\delta)$, times a bracket. The bracket starts from
the same effective rate, which already nets out that a persistently high flow value raises
the hiring cost a continuing match saves, through $\rho(1-\bar\tau)$. Two persistence terms
then add to it: part of that saving goes to the worker, $\rho\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f$, and a
matched firm forgoes the higher flow value, $\rho(1-\phi)(1-\bar\delta)\bar q$. At $\rho=0$,
$a=-\bigl[\bar\delta/(r+\bar\delta)+\bar\delta/(1-\bar\delta)\bigr]/\eta_L$.

The numerator of $b$ is only $\rho(1-\bar\delta)\bar s$: a persistent rise in separations
lowers the probability that next period's match continues. There is no flow-value term,
because $s$ does not depreciate the vacancy. This is the source of the asymmetry between the
two shocks.

\begin{remark}[Block recursivity]\label{rem:recursive}
Given Lemma~\ref{lem:theta}, the states are solved backward. Equation \eqref{eq:l_u} gives
$\hat u$, and \eqref{eq:l_theta} gives $\hat v=\hat\theta+\hat u$. The predetermined stock
$v_{pre,t}=(1-\delta_{t-1})\{[1-q(\theta_{t-1})]\theta_{t-1}u_{t-1}+s_{t-1}(1-u_{t-1})\}$
depends only on $(u_{t-1},\theta_{t-1},\delta_{t-1},s_{t-1})$ and has no own lag. It feeds
into nothing except entry, $e_t=v_t-v_{pre,t}$. The system thus has one unstable root,
$1/\Phi$, for one jump variable, and one stable root, $\lambda$, for one dynamic state, $u$,
so the Blanchard--Kahn condition holds.

The structure breaks with finite $\xi$. Entry $e=G(Q)$ then links $v_{pre}$ to $Q$, and $Q$
enters the job creation condition. Tightness depends on the states, the policy rule must
include state coefficients, and the closed form is lost. The same happens when the
conditions of \texttt{prop:independence} fail, through $N$, $C$ or $\chi^c$.
\end{remark}

For each shock $x\in\{\delta,s\}$, write $c_x$ for its tightness coefficient
($c_\delta=a$, $c_s=b$) and $\ell_x$ for its unemployment loading in \eqref{eq:l_u},
\[
	\ell_\delta\equiv\bar f\,\frac{\bar\delta}{\bar\tau},
	\qquad
	\ell_s\equiv(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\Bigl(1-\frac{\bar\delta}{\bar\tau}\Bigr),
\]
and, when $c_x\ne0$, define the ratio of the unemployment loading to the tightness loading,
\[
	R_x\equiv\frac{\ell_x}{\lvert c_x\rvert}.
\]
In the CK case, $\bar\delta=\bar\tau$, so $R_\delta=\bar f/\lvert a\rvert$ and there is no
separation margin ($\bar s=0$).

\begin{proposition}[Destruction and separation shocks under infinitely elastic vacancy creation]
\label{prop:fe}
Under the assumptions of Lemma~\ref{lem:theta}, consider an innovation $\varepsilon_0>0$ to
shock $x\in\{\delta,s\}$. Let $\lambda\equiv1-(1-\bar\delta)\bar f-\bar\tau\in[0,1)$ be the
persistence of unemployment, one minus the effective job-finding rate and the separation
rate, and let $\omega\equiv(1-\eta_L)(1-\bar\delta)\bar f$ be the effective job-finding rate
times its elasticity to tightness. To first order,
\[
	\hat\theta_t=c_x\,\rho^t\varepsilon_0,
	\qquad
	\hat u_t=\bigl(\ell_x+\omega\lvert c_x\rvert\bigr)\varepsilon_0\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\lambda^{t-1-j}\rho^j,
	\qquad
	\hat v_t=\hat\theta_t+\hat u_t ,
\]
so unemployment is unchanged on impact and rises thereafter. Moreover, when $c_x\ne0$:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Vacancies fall at $h=1$ if and only if
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:H1}
		\rho>\omega+R_x .
	\end{equation}
	Once vacancies rise above steady state, they stay above it.
	\item The slope of $v_t-\bar v$ on $u_t-\bar u$ along the impulse response is
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:slope}
		\bar\theta\left[1-\frac{\rho(1-\lambda^2)}{(1+\rho\lambda)(\omega+R_x)}\right],
	\end{equation}
	which is negative if and only if
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:HP}
		\frac{\rho(1-\lambda^2)}{1+\rho\lambda}>\omega+R_x .
	\end{equation}
	\item For the separation shock, $R_s=(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\,D/(\rho\bar\tau)$. If
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:s_bound}
		(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\Bigl[\eta_L+\frac{\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f}{\bar\tau}\Bigr]\ge1,
	\end{equation}
	then after a separation shock $\hat v_t>0$ for every $t\ge1$ and the slope is positive,
	for every $\rho\in(0,1)$.
\end{enumerate}
If $\rho=0$, both conditions fail for the destruction shock, and the separation shock leaves
tightness unchanged at every date. In both cases $v_t=\bar\theta u_t$ after impact and the
slope equals $\bar\theta>0$.
\end{proposition}

Both conditions compare the persistence of the shock with the same threshold,
$\omega+R_x$. At $h=1$, the tightness fall that survives one period, $\lvert c_x\rvert\rho$,
must exceed the rise in unemployment. That rise has two parts: the hires lost at date 0,
$\omega\lvert c_x\rvert$, and the direct effect of the shock, $\ell_x=R_x\lvert c_x\rvert$.
So $\rho>\omega$ is necessary, and $R_x$ is the extra persistence needed to outweigh the
direct effect. Over the whole path, persistence is discounted by unemployment's own
persistence, since $\rho(1-\lambda^2)/(1+\rho\lambda)<\rho$, so \eqref{eq:HP} is the
stricter condition. The two shocks differ only through $R_x$. Condition \eqref{eq:s_bound}
says that job finding is fast relative to separation and that workers have some
bargaining power, so that wage feedback cushions tightness.

\begin{proof}
The innovation sets $\tilde x_t=\rho^t\varepsilon_0$ and the other shock to zero, so by
Lemma~\ref{lem:theta} $\hat\theta_t=c_x\rho^t\varepsilon_0$.

\emph{Step 1: unemployment.} $u_0$ is predetermined, so $\hat u_0=0$. Substituting
$\hat\theta_{t-1}=-\lvert c_x\rvert\rho^{t-1}\varepsilon_0$ and
$\tilde x_{t-1}=\rho^{t-1}\varepsilon_0$ into \eqref{eq:l_u},
\[
	\hat u_t=\lambda\hat u_{t-1}+\ell_x\rho^{t-1}\varepsilon_0
	+\omega\lvert c_x\rvert\rho^{t-1}\varepsilon_0
	=\lambda\hat u_{t-1}+\bigl(\ell_x+\omega\lvert c_x\rvert\bigr)\rho^{t-1}\varepsilon_0 .
\]
Iterating from $\hat u_0=0$ gives $\hat u_1=(\ell_x+\omega\lvert c_x\rvert)\varepsilon_0$,
$\hat u_2=(\ell_x+\omega\lvert c_x\rvert)(\lambda+\rho)\varepsilon_0$, and in general
$\hat u_t=(\ell_x+\omega\lvert c_x\rvert)\varepsilon_0\,g_t$ with
$g_t\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\lambda^{t-1-j}\rho^j$. Since $\lambda,\rho\ge0$ and $g_1=1$,
$\hat u_t>0$ for $t\ge1$. By \eqref{eq:l_theta}, $\hat v_t=\hat\theta_t+\hat u_t$. When
$c_x\ne0$, $\ell_x+\omega\lvert c_x\rvert=\lvert c_x\rvert(\omega+R_x)$.

\emph{Step 2: part 1.} With $g_1=1$,
\[
	\hat v_1=-\lvert c_x\rvert\rho\varepsilon_0+\lvert c_x\rvert(\omega+R_x)\varepsilon_0
	=\lvert c_x\rvert\bigl(\omega+R_x-\rho\bigr)\varepsilon_0,
\]
which is negative if and only if \eqref{eq:H1} holds. For $t\ge1$ and $\rho>0$, divide
$\hat v_t=\lvert c_x\rvert\bigl[-\rho^t+(\omega+R_x)g_t\bigr]\varepsilon_0$ by
$\lvert c_x\rvert\rho^t\varepsilon_0>0$, and use $g_t/\rho^t=\rho^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}(\lambda/\rho)^i$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vt_neg}
	\hat v_t<0
	\iff
	\rho>(\omega+R_x)\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\Bigl(\frac{\lambda}{\rho}\Bigr)^i .
\end{equation}
The right side is strictly increasing in $t$. So once the inequality fails, it fails at all
later dates.

\emph{Step 3: part 2.} In levels,
$u_t-\bar u=\bar u\lvert c_x\rvert(\omega+R_x)\varepsilon_0\,g_t$ and
$v_t-\bar v=\bar v\lvert c_x\rvert\bigl[-\rho^t+(\omega+R_x)g_t\bigr]\varepsilon_0$, with
$g_0=0$. For $\rho\ne\lambda$, $g_t=(\rho^t-\lambda^t)/(\rho-\lambda)$; the case
$\rho=\lambda$ follows by continuity. Summing geometric series,
\[
	\sum_{t\ge0}\rho^tg_t=\frac{\rho}{(1-\rho^2)(1-\rho\lambda)},\qquad
	\sum_{t\ge0}g_t^2=\frac{1+\rho\lambda}{(1-\rho^2)(1-\lambda^2)(1-\rho\lambda)} .
\]
The first uses $\frac{1}{1-\rho^2}-\frac{1}{1-\rho\lambda}
=\frac{\rho(\rho-\lambda)}{(1-\rho^2)(1-\rho\lambda)}$. The second uses
$(1-\rho\lambda)(1-\lambda^2)-2(1-\rho^2)(1-\lambda^2)+(1-\rho^2)(1-\rho\lambda)
=(\rho-\lambda)^2(1+\rho\lambda)$. Therefore
\[
	\frac{\sum_t(v_t-\bar v)(u_t-\bar u)}{\sum_t(u_t-\bar u)^2}
	=\frac{\bar v}{\bar u}\Bigl[1-\frac{1}{\omega+R_x}\,\frac{\sum\rho^tg_t}{\sum g_t^2}\Bigr]
	=\bar\theta\Bigl[1-\frac{\rho(1-\lambda^2)}{(1+\rho\lambda)(\omega+R_x)}\Bigr],
\]
which is \eqref{eq:slope}. It is negative if and only if \eqref{eq:HP} holds.

\emph{Step 4: part 3.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:theta}, $\lvert b\rvert=\rho(1-\bar\delta)\bar s/D$.
Using $(1-\bar\delta)\bar s=\bar\tau-\bar\delta=\bar\tau(1-\bar\delta/\bar\tau)$,
\[
	R_s=\frac{(1-\bar\delta)\bar f(1-\bar\delta/\bar\tau)\,D}{\rho\,\bar\tau(1-\bar\delta/\bar\tau)}
	=\frac{(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\,D}{\rho\,\bar\tau}.
\]
Since $(1+r)/\rho>1+r$,
\[
	\frac{D}{\rho}=\eta_L\Bigl[\frac{1+r}{\rho}-(1-\bar\tau)\Bigr]+\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f
	>\eta_L(r+\bar\tau)+\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f,
\]
so
\[
	R_s>(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\Bigl[\eta_L\frac{r+\bar\tau}{\bar\tau}+\frac{\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f}{\bar\tau}\Bigr]
	>(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\Bigl[\eta_L+\frac{\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f}{\bar\tau}\Bigr]\ge1
\]
under \eqref{eq:s_bound}. Then for $t\ge1$, $(\omega+R_s)g_t>g_t\ge\rho^{t-1}\ge\rho^t$, because
$g_t$ contains the term $\rho^{t-1}$, so $\hat v_t=\lvert b\rvert[-\rho^t+(\omega+R_s)g_t]\varepsilon_0>0$.
And $\rho(1-\lambda^2)/(1+\rho\lambda)<\rho<1<\omega+R_s$, so the slope \eqref{eq:slope} is
positive.

\emph{Step 5: $\rho=0$.} For the destruction shock, the left sides of \eqref{eq:H1} and
\eqref{eq:HP} are zero while $\omega+R_\delta>0$, so both conditions fail. Since
$\hat\theta_t=a\cdot0^t\varepsilon_0=0$ for $t\ge1$, $\hat v_t=\hat u_t$ and
$v_t=\bar\theta u_t$ after impact, and the slope \eqref{eq:slope} is $\bar\theta$. For the
separation shock, $b=0$, so $\hat\theta_t=0$ at every date and $\hat v_t=\hat u_t$
throughout. The slope is again $\bar\theta$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Vacancies below steady state at every horizon]\label{cor:allh}
Under the assumptions of Proposition~\ref{prop:fe}, with $c_x\ne0$, $\hat v_t<0$ for every
$t\ge0$ if and only if $\rho-\lambda\ge\omega+R_x$. This requires $\rho>\lambda+\omega$,
which is $0.72$ at our calibration.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
In \eqref{eq:vt_neg}, if $\rho\le\lambda$ the right side grows without bound, so vacancies
eventually rise above steady state. If $\rho>\lambda$ it converges from below to
$(\omega+R_x)\rho/(\rho-\lambda)$, so the inequality holds at every $t$ if and only if
$\rho-\lambda\ge\omega+R_x$.
\end{proof}

\section{Interpretation}

\paragraph{Why tightness falls}
At $\rho=0$, \eqref{eq:l_jcc} reads
$\eta_L\hat\theta_0=-[\bar\delta/(r+\bar\delta)+\bar\delta/(1-\bar\delta)]\tilde\delta_0$. The
flow value of a vacancy, and hence the cost of a hire, rises by $\bar\delta/(r+\bar\delta)$
percent per percent of $\delta$, which is $0.45$ at D1. The value of a hire falls only
through survival, by the small amount $\bar\delta/(1-\bar\delta)$. For the two to stay
equal, the expected time to fill, $1/q$, must fall, so tightness falls.

\paragraph{The iid case}
Tightness and vacancies fall only at date 0, while unemployment is predetermined. At date 1
the flow value is back to $\bar K$ and $\theta_1=\bar\theta$. Unemployment rises, and
$v_1=\bar\theta u_1$ rises with it as entry refills the stock. In the $(u,v)$ plane the
economy drops straight down, jumps up and to the right, and returns along the ray
$v=\bar\theta u$. At D1, of the $0.16\%$ rise in $\hat u_1$ per 1\% shock, $0.04$ comes from
destruction, $(\bar f\bar\delta/\bar\tau)\tilde\delta_0$, and $0.12$ from the date-0 collapse
in hiring, $\omega\lvert\hat\theta_0\rvert$.

\paragraph{The persistent case}
Tightness stays low while destruction stays high, so vacancies stay below steady state
while unemployment builds up. Once tightness has recovered enough, $v=\theta u$ turns
positive and stays positive.

\paragraph{Where $\bar\delta$ relative to $\bar\tau$ enters}
For the destruction shock, $\bar\delta$ enters both conditions only through $R_\delta$, the
ratio of the unemployment loading, $\bar f\bar\delta/\bar\tau$, to the tightness loading,
$\lvert a\rvert$. The second is roughly proportional to $\bar\delta/(r+\bar\delta)$, so
$R_\delta$ is roughly proportional to $(r+\bar\delta)/\bar\tau$. Lowering $\bar\delta$ relative
to $\bar\tau$ shrinks the unemployment loading in proportion, while the tightness loading
shrinks much less (Table~\ref{tab:weights}), so $R_\delta$ falls. At D1,
$(r+\bar\delta)/\bar\tau=0.19$ and $R_\delta=0.045$, so the threshold $\omega+R_\delta$ is
barely above $\omega=0.164$. With $\bar\delta=\bar\tau$ (CK, $s=0$),
$(r+\bar\delta)/\bar\tau=1.11$ and $R_\delta=\bar f/\lvert a\rvert=0.289$: the vacancy dies
with the match, so its flow value cannot respond much more than unemployment does, and the
threshold rises to $0.453$.

\paragraph{The destruction--separation asymmetry}
The two shocks share the denominator $D$ of their tightness coefficients and the
thresholds of Proposition~\ref{prop:fe}. They differ in $R_x$. A destruction shock
depreciates the vacancy asset, so tightness moves through the flow-value term
$\bar\delta/(r+\bar\delta)$, and at D1 its unemployment loading is small because destruction
is a small share of separations. A separation shock leaves the vacancy's flow value
unchanged and moves tightness only through the probability that a match continues, while
its unemployment loading is large because separations are most of the flows. At D1 and
$\rho=0.592$, $R_\delta=0.045$ against $R_s=9.56$. Both persistence terms are below one, so
the separation shock gives positive comovement at every persistence, while the destruction
shock gives negative comovement once it is moderately persistent. This is the free-entry
counterpart of \texttt{prop:ds\_asymmetry}, with closed-form conditions on both sides.

\section{Remarks}

\begin{remark}[Holding $K$ fixed]\label{rem:flow}
If the vacancy cost were a constant flow, rather than the flow value of an asset that
$\delta_t$ depreciates, the $\bar\delta/(r+\bar\delta)$ terms would drop out of
\eqref{eq:l_jcc} and \eqref{eq:a}. Only the survival effect would remain, and
$\lvert a\rvert=(1+r)[\bar\delta/(1-\bar\delta)]/\{\eta_L[1+r-\rho(1-\bar\tau)]+\rho\phi(1-\bar\delta)\bar f\}$ is
small. At our calibration the path slope is then positive in every economy (about $0.49$
at $\rho=0.592$). This is the flow-cost DMP case. The separation shock never moves the
flow value, so for it the durable vacancy and the flow-cost vacancy give the same tightness
response.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
A permanent shock ($\rho\to1$) recovers the steady-state comparison. It hides the last
statement of Proposition~\ref{prop:fe}: without persistence, the response is positively
sloped after impact.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
\texttt{prop:ds\_asymmetry} Part~3 in the draft gives ``$\bar\delta_e/\bar\tau$ small'' as a
sufficient condition. In the iid free-entry case, entry refills the stock at $t+1$, and
that condition fails for every $\bar\delta_e/\bar\tau$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
Part~3 of Proposition~\ref{prop:fe} is consistent with \texttt{prop:ds\_asymmetry} Part~1
in the draft, and extends it. Part~1 signs the separation shock at $h=1$ under conditions on
entry, and solves the iid free-entry case exactly (its Step~4(a), where tightness stays at
$\bar\theta$). Here the sign holds at every horizon and for every persistence, under
\eqref{eq:s_bound}.
\end{remark}
